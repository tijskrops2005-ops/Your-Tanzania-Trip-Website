Standing 5,895 metres (19,340 ft) tall, Mount Kilimanjaro is Africa’s highest mountain and the largest freestanding mountain on earth, a bright landmark on the map of Tanzania. If a trip to this beautiful country is on your list, you may be asking yourself – how hard is it to climb Mount Kilimanjaro?

Below we explain clearly how difficult the Kilimanjaro hike is, what lies ahead of you and how to give yourself the best possible chance of reaching the summit. One thing we can say straight away – the trek is far from easy, but it is well worth the effort.

Is Kilimanjaro Hard To Climb?

The short answer is yes. A Kilimanjaro trek is no walk in the park for most people. That said, the routes are not especially hard in themselves. Most treks involve no steep or technical climbing, yet that is not where the real difficulty lies.

What really tests you is the altitude. Close to the summit there is only half as much oxygen as at sea level, and without care this can put your body under heavy strain.

Cold can be another obstacle. Contrary to what many people expect from Africa, temperatures on the hike can fall quite low.

On top of this, hiking Kilimanjaro means sleeping in tents and getting by with very few facilities for a minimum of five to six days.

So although it is not very demanding on a technical level, most people who have done the Kilimanjaro hike describe it as hard.

   

Mount Kilimanjaro Difficulty

Altitude on Kilimanjaro

The problem hikers on Kilimanjaro run into most often is altitude, or to be exact, the effect of low oxygen levels on the body. On the summit of Kilimanjaro you get roughly 49% of the oxygen normally available at sea level. It quite literally leaves you breathless.

Oxygen levels this low raise the chance of altitude sickness, which can show itself through a range of unpleasant (and at times potentially dangerous) symptoms, including dizziness, headaches, nausea and shortness of breath.

That is why a key factor in a successful climb is acclimatization. Some climbers do dedicated altitude training to get ready for the trek, although this is not essential.

What you can do is opt for a longer itinerary, taking six to nine days to reach Uhuru Peak. The more time you allow, the better. Your body then has the chance to adapt gradually to the reduced oxygen and to make more red blood cells, so oxygen is spread through your body more efficiently.

Because it is so extremely high, the summit of Kilimanjaro has no plants or animals. It also means that staying in this environment for long is unsafe, particularly if you have not acclimatised properly.

So if you would like to take on the mountain, we strongly advise picking a longer route.

Kilimanjaro Hike Routes

Eight Kilimanjaro routes take you to the summit:

Route Distance Time
Umbwe 53 km (32 miles) 5 – 7 days
The Western Breach 52 km (32 miles) 6 – 7 days
Shira 56 km (35 miles) 6 – 8 days
Machame 62 km (37 miles) 6 – 7 days
Rongai 70 km (43 miles) 6 – 7 days
Lemosho 70 km (42 miles) 7 – 9 days
Marangu 72 km (45 miles) 5 – 6 days
Northern Circuit 88 km (53 miles) 8 – 9 days

Depending on the route you pick, you will trek for five to nine days straight. As mentioned earlier, the longer treks (Machame, Lemosho, Rongai or Northern Circuit), which need 6+ days to complete, are easier and safer for your body.

Some companies promote and run short routes, which are mainly popular because they cost less. It is worth knowing, though, that the success rate on these treks is much lower (around 60%) than on the longer routes, where it reaches 85% to 95%.

The Western Breach route is by far the hardest and most dangerous on Kilimanjaro. This is because of its steep, rocky slope, which is known to cause boulder avalanches that have injured at least six people over the years.

For beginners, the best trek is the Lemosho route, thanks to its long acclimatisation period and relatively easy walking. It also ranks among the most scenic routes.

The Marangu route, also known as the Coca-Cola route, is another sound option if you are climbing Kilimanjaro for the first time. It has huts rather than tents and appeals to novice trekkers because it is not very difficult. As you may have guessed, that also makes it one of the most crowded treks.

You do not need specialised equipment such as ropes, harnesses or axes on any of the Kilimanjaro routes. That is not to say the trek has no tough sections. For most of the way, however, the hike is within reach of people with very different levels of mountaineering experience.

Camping and Sleeping Conditions

You should also think about where you will sleep on the Kilimanjaro hike. On every route apart from Marangu, hikers sleep in tents. Depending on how comfortable you are with that and what you expect, this may be perfectly fine or something you would rather not put up with.

Keep in mind – the trek takes a minimum of five to six days, and for all that time you will sleep on the ground in a sleeping bag with other hikers around you. If that does not appeal to you, choose the Marangu route.

There are no facilities such as showers at all. So no, you will not be able to wash properly while you are on the hike (although nobody really minds). There are toilets, though, and they are cleaned regularly – even if on the busiest routes they may not be in ideal condition.

Weather on Kilimanjaro

The weather on Kilimanjaro is fairly good, particularly in the dry seasons. The safest and most comfortable periods for hiking Kilimanjaro are July to October and December to February.

Even so, although the mountain lies in Africa, the altitude means night-time temperatures can fall very low (as far as -20 degrees Celsius). Being prepared for this is therefore essential.

Bringing and wearing protective clothing and sleeping in a good-quality, well-insulated sleeping bag will make the experience far more comfortable.

High-altitude mountaineering always carries the risk of bad weather, and Kilimanjaro is no exception. The good news is that experienced guides will accompany you throughout your hike and do all they can to keep you safe and comfortable.

Your Physical and Mental Condition

So how fit must you be to climb Kilimanjaro? The easiest way to answer this is to look at what kind of challenge the Kilimanjaro trek actually is.

On the hike you will walk over uneven and at times steep ground for five to nine days in a row.

Among the toughest parts of the entire trek is the summit night, when you face a long, hard climb at high altitude. Once you have reached the top, you also need to descend again, all within a single day. That adds up to up to 15 hours of trekking over fairly demanding terrain and in tough conditions.

The Kilimanjaro climb can also take a mental and emotional toll. You are a long way from home, without your usual comforts, sleeping in a tent, feeling the cold and relying on the people you are hiking with.

Preparing both body and mind for this adventure is wise. An intensive training programme (at least four days of sessions to build stamina and strength) may help you.

Make sure, too, that your motivation is rock solid. Choose a trustworthy company to arrange your hike, so you can feel safe and at ease knowing you are in good hands.

How hard is Kilimanjaro? A look at success rates

As noted above, the longer routes have the best success rates. The Lemosho route, for example, has an impressive 90% success rate, while the apparently more popular Marangu route reaches only 65%.

On average, around 65% of climbers succeed on Mount Kilimanjaro, so nearly half of everyone who attempts this mountain does not make it.

Is Kilimanjaro within reach of an average person?

Yes, an average, active person can certainly climb Kilimanjaro. Several factors contribute to a successful ascent.

  • Good preparation and planning ahead
  • An experienced, supportive guide (we will arrange this)
  • Choosing a longer trekking route
  • Strong motivation and a positive mindset

Some FAQ at a glance

How many climbers do not reach the top of Kilimanjaro?

Somewhere between a third and a half of climbers do not reach the summit of Mount Kilimanjaro. The reasons vary, but the main one is the altitude and the harmful effect of low oxygen levels on the body.

Is 50 too old to climb Kilimanjaro?

No, Kilimanjaro can be climbed by anyone who is physically active, strong enough and healthy. Age is not a major factor.

Can a beginner climb Kilimanjaro?

Yes, Kilimanjaro is open to beginners. What matters is getting ready for the challenge. You should be in good physical shape, ideally after training for at least a few months before the trek.
