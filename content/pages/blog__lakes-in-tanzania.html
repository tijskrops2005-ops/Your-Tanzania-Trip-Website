Tanzania’s Lakes: Explore the Beauty and Diversity of Inland Water

  • Home
  • Blog
  • Tanzania’s Lakes: Explore the Beauty and Diversity of Inland Water

10 July 2024

Few lakes anywhere in the world are as spectacular and varied as those of Tanzania. Even better, they offer a whole range of things to do and see, from wildlife spotting and fishing to swimming and kayaking. And, as lakes do so well, they are also a vital source of water for local communities and ecosystems. In this article we’ll share everything we know about Tanzania’s breathtaking lakes!

 

Lake Victoria – Africa’s largest lake

Lake Victoria is a natural wonder and a refuge for a remarkable range of wildlife and intriguing features. This enormous expanse of water, shared by Tanzania, Uganda and Kenya, is not only Africa’s largest lake but also the largest tropical lake in the world by surface area. For all its size, it is fairly shallow: the average depth is just 41 metres, which lets sunlight reach down and feed a rich ecosystem beneath the surface.

Countless fish species live in the lake, most notably over 500 kinds of cichlid, many of them found nowhere else. These cichlids are famous for their varied looks and behaviour, bringing colour and life to the underwater world. The shoreline is every bit as lively as the water. The iconic hippopotamus is regularly seen lazing in the shallows or basking on the banks, alongside plenty of companions such as the African clawless otter (YES otters 😍), the marsh mongoose, sitatunga, bohor reedbuck, defassa waterbuck and cane rats.

Reptiles flourish here too, with the formidable Nile crocodile stealing the show. It shares the lake with several turtle species, among them the African helmeted turtle, the Williams’ mud turtle and the variable mud turtle, all of them unique to Lake Victoria. More of a bird person? Then get ready to smile: more than 300 bird species live around Lake Victoria, including the African marsh harrier, the mysterious Shoebill, herons, geese, grey parrots, kingfishers and the fish eagle.

Adding to the lake’s appeal are its more than 3,000 islets, among them the well-known Ssese Islands and Ukerewe Island. Every one of them has its own blend of scenery and cultural experiences to offer. Given this combination of rich biodiversity, fascinating culture and stunning beauty, it is hardly surprising that Lake Victoria sits at the top of so many bucket lists.

A few facts about Lake Victoria to impress your fellow travellers with:

  • The explorer John Hanning Speke named it after Queen Victoria of England in 1858.
  • To local people it is Nnalubaale, Luganda for “the giver of life”.

Lake Tanganyika – the longest lake on earth

At a remarkable 626 kilometres (420 miles) long and 50 kilometres (31 miles wide, Lake Tanganyika is the longest lake in the world and the second-largest body of freshwater by volume. Tucked between Tanzania, Burundi, Zambia and the Democratic Republic of Congo, this magnificent lake holds a wealth of wonders.

Tanganyika is known for its astonishingly clear blue water and sandy beaches scattered with palms and acacia trees. Beneath the surface thrives a lively ecosystem of over 250 species of fish and other aquatic life, many of which exist nowhere else on Earth. Hippo families wallow in the shallows while crocodiles cruise just off the shore. Birds are everywhere, from brightly coloured kingfishers to huge fish eagles that plunge down to seize their catch.

Remarkably, Lake Tanganyika holds close to 16% of all the available surface freshwater on Earth. Rivers feed it, and its outlet drains into the Congo River system. This is a lake overflowing with beauty and life, and a visit will leave you with memories to last a lifetime.

Lake Malawi – The Meromictic Lake

Lake Malawi is genuinely unique. With a surface of over 29,000 square kilometres, it is Africa’s third-largest lake and the ninth-largest on the planet. What sets Lake Malawi apart, though, is that it is one of the very few meromictic lakes on Earth. Meromixis means the lake’s layers of water don’t mix: the warmer, oxygen-rich upper layer seldom blends with the colder, saltier and less oxygenated layer below.

The reason lies in Lake Malawi’s great depth of up to 700 metres, its unusual river inflows and the geology of the valley around it. The surrounding highlands also supply the lake with more nutrients than it can absorb, so that very little water circulates.

Lake Malawi’s varied aquatic habitats sustain an exceptionally rich biodiversity. More than 1,000 species of colourful cichlid fish live in the lake, and new ones are still being found. These cichlids have adapted in distinctive ways to the many niches and microclimates within the lake. Other species unique to the lake include crabs, sponges, shrimp and jellyfish. Lake Malawi is, in effect, a freshwater coral reef, with more fish species than any other lake on Earth.

Lake Rukwa – The Fluctuating Lake

Tucked into the lovely Southern Highlands of Tanzania, Lake Rukwa is an intriguing lake that goes through extreme seasonal swings. In the rainy season Lake Rukwa swells into a vast inland sea of more than 2,600 square kilometres. Once the dry season sets in, however, the lake shrinks dramatically and leaves a web of swamps and channels in its wake.

These shifting water levels deeply affect both how the lake looks and the animals that live in and around it. At high water the lake is full of fish and aquatic plants. Hippos loll in the water while crocodiles hover just beneath the surface, and more than 400 bird species gather at Lake Rukwa to feed and nest among the papyrus reeds.

In the dry season, though, it looks completely different: as the water retreats, wide plains emerge and the lake becomes a broad floodplain scattered with pools and wetlands. The animals adapt in surprising ways. Fish shelter in small channels and pools. Crocodiles dig into mud dens and slip into a state much like hibernation. Hippos cope with the crowding in the pools that remain, and birds gather wherever water is still to be found.

In spite of these harsh swings, Lake Rukwa continues to be an ecosystem of vital importance to Tanzania.

Lake Natron – The Red Lake

Lake Natron ranks among the most unusual bodies of water in Tanzania. It lies in the north of the country, close to the Kenyan border, and is on average just 3 metres deep. What makes Natron so extraordinary, however, is its strong alkalinity: the pH of the water can climb to a staggering 12.

This extremely alkaline environment is the result of rapid evaporation and the lake’s position in an active volcanic region. The alkalinity stems from sodium carbonate and other minerals carried into the lake by small rivers and hot springs.

Because of this high alkalinity, Lake Natron is hostile to most forms of life. Yet a handful of extremophile organisms have adapted in remarkable ways.

  • Spirulina algae flourish here and give the water its red-orange tint. These algae can survive complete dehydration and revive afterwards.
  • Tilapia live close to freshwater hot springs, where the alkalinity is lower, and a coating of mucus shields them from burns.
  • Flamingos adapted to Natron feed on the spirulina: their intricate bills strain out the algae, while their long legs keep their bodies clear of the caustic surface.

Beyond these intriguing creatures lies an astonishing fact: Lake Natron is the only breeding ground in East Africa for a remarkable population of 2.5 million lesser flamingoes. Their “near threatened” status is closely bound up with their dependence on this single site, which makes the lake an irreplaceable refuge for these majestic birds.

Lake Eyasi – The Salt Lake

Lake Eyasi is a large salt lake lying at the foot of the Great Rift Valley, directly south of the Serengeti National Park and to the southwest of the Ngorongoro Crater.

Ringed by steep escarpments and savannah grassland, this distinctive lake is notable for its high salinity and varied birdlife. Years of constant evaporation under the hot tropical sun have concentrated minerals and salts in the water, giving it a milky, cloudy look. The shallow water spreads over more than 400 square kilometres, although the level of the lake varies greatly with the rainfall.

The high salt content has produced a peculiar ecosystem that copes with the arid conditions. Algae and microorganisms thrive in the mineral-rich water, and as a result large flocks of waterbirds head to Lake Eyasi to feast on them.

Flamingos, pelicans, storks and ducks relax on the muddy shores while birds of prey such as fish eagles wheel overhead. On the grassy plains around the lake you will find grazing mammals including zebras, antelopes and baboons.

In this unique, ancient landscape, where high salinity and varied birdlife are the norm, Lake Eyasi’s importance reaches well beyond its natural wonders. Archaeological finds around the lake point to early human settlements going back 200,000 years.

Lake Manyara – The Flamingo Lake

Lying at the foot of the Great Rift Valley escarpment, Lake Manyara is a shallow alkaline lake covering 230 square kilometres. It may be modest in size, but it makes up for it with beauty and an abundance of wildlife.

Manyara earned its nickname, the “Flamingo Lake”, from the huge numbers of Lesser Flamingos that live here. Roughly over 400,000 of these glossy pink birds gather on Manyara’s marshy shores to feed on algae and to breed. A flamingo ballet is pure magic to watch: thousands of spindly legs wading in step through blue water, necks dipping together as they filter feed.

Flamingos aside, over 400 bird species visit the lake, among them pelicans, storks, cormorants, kingfishers, eagles and vultures. The acacia woodland around the shore draws babblers, weavers, hornbills and starlings as well. Is Manyara a paradise for birdwatchers? Without a doubt!

For visitors, Lake Manyara National Park provides superb wildlife viewing straight from your vehicle or on a bush walk. Tree-climbing lions doze on the branches while hippos relax in the pools. Blue monkeys dart through the forest and thousands of flamingos paint the shoreline pink.

The lakes of Tanzania really do combine natural beauty, rich biodiversity and cultural significance in a way all their own. Whether you watch the flamingo ballet at Lake Manyara, discover the underwater world of Lake Tanganyika or marvel at the extraordinary ecosystem of Lake Natron, every lake sends you home with memories for life.
