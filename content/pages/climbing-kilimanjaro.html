This page is for anyone thinking about Kilimanjaro: which route, which month, how fit you need to be and what it costs. The climbs above are the ones we run most; every one of them can be adjusted in length, and all of them can be combined with a safari or Zanzibar.

Kilimanjaro itineraries

Seven routes lead to the summit. Six start on the southern or western side of the mountain; Rongai starts in the north, near the Kenyan border. We offer climbs on all of them, in five to nine days. The route you pick decides how much scenery you see, how many other climbers you meet, whether you sleep in tents or huts, and above all how much time your body gets to adjust to the altitude.

Kilimanjaro routes at a glance

First climb, no mountain experience: Machame in 7 days, or Marangu if you prefer huts
Scenery: Lemosho or Machame
Smallest budget: Marangu, 5 or 6 days
Fewest other climbers: Lemosho, Rongai or the Northern Circuit
Experienced hikers who want it steep: Umbwe
Most chosen on this site: Machame, 7 days
Longest and gentlest: Northern Circuit, 8 or 9 days
Rainy season: Rongai, the driest side of the mountain
Best chance of the summit: Lemosho in 8 days or the Northern Circuit in 9

Kilimanjaro routes: what to expect

Each route has a different mix of length, difficulty, scenery, crowds and accommodation. Below are the three our guests choose most. Ask us about the others; Baraka's guides have led groups on all seven.

Machame Route

Machame, the so-called Whiskey Route, is the most walked route on the mountain and the one we recommend to most first-time climbers in its seven-day version. You climb up through rainforest to the Shira plateau, then loop around the south side of the summit cone, past the Lava Tower and the Barranco Wall, before the night walk to Stella Point and Uhuru Peak. The loop is what makes it work: you climb high and sleep low, which gives your body time to adjust.

The last stretch from Barafu to the crater rim is steep and starts around midnight. You need a basic level of fitness and the patience to walk very slowly. In six days the route is possible; in seven, in our experience, far more climbers reach the top.

For: varied scenery, good acclimatisation profile, and a lower price than the longer routes because the start is close to Moshi.

Against: busy in the dry season, especially at the camps.

Marangu Route

Marangu, nicknamed the Coca-Cola Route, is the only route with huts instead of tents: dormitory bunks, a dining hall and a roof when it rains. It is also the shortest route, and that is its weakness. With five days up and down, your body gets little time to acclimatise, and the summit rate is lower than on the longer routes. We suggest the six-day version with an extra night at Horombo if you choose Marangu.

You walk up and down the same trail, so the scenery repeats. In return it is the cheapest way up the mountain and the easiest on the logistics.

For: huts, the lowest price, the shortest time away.

Against: less acclimatisation, the same trail twice, and crowded huts in high season.

Lemosho Route

Lemosho starts on the western side of the mountain, further from the towns, and that keeps the first days quiet. The trail crosses the Shira plateau with wide views before joining the Machame Route at the Lava Tower. In seven or eight days it has one of the best summit rates on the mountain, because the ascent is spread out so evenly.

For: scenery, few other climbers in the first days, and time to acclimatise.

Against: the price. The route is long and the trailhead is a longer drive, which adds transport costs and crew days.

Best time to climb Kilimanjaro

You can climb in any month; the conditions differ. The two dry periods are the most comfortable: January to early March, which is the warmest and clearest, and June to October, which is colder and busier. February is the month with the lowest chance of rain.

April, May and November are the wet months. The trails are muddy, the views come and go, and the summit night can bring snow. Some climbers choose these months on purpose for the empty camps and the lower prices; Rongai, on the drier northern side, is the route we suggest then. Whatever the month, summit night is well below freezing.

How to prepare

A climbing day means six to seven hours of walking with a daypack, several days in a row, and summit night is twelve hours or more. You do not need to be an athlete, but you do need stamina. In the months before the trip, walk: long hikes every other weekend, with the boots you will wear on the mountain, until a full weekend of hiking feels normal.

Altitude is the other half. Nobody can train for it at sea level, so the route and its length do that work for you; that is why we steer most climbers to seven or eight days. On the mountain, drink a lot, walk slowly and tell your guide how you feel. The guides check everyone twice a day.

What it costs

The price depends on the route, the number of days and your group size; the climbs above show the rate per person for 2, 4 and 6 climbers in each season. The crew, tents, meals, park fees and transfers are in the price. On top of that, budget for:

  • Gear you do not own yet: boots, a warm sleeping bag, layers, poles. Most of it can be rented in Arusha; we tell you what is worth buying.
  • Travel insurance that covers trekking above 5,000 metres and helicopter evacuation.
  • Tips for the guides, cook and porters. Tipping is customary on Kilimanjaro; we give you the usual amounts per crew member before you go.

How we climb responsibly

Every climb is run by our partner Kilimanjaro Nature Tours in Arusha. Their guides are licensed by the Kilimanjaro National Park authority and trained in Wilderness First Response, first aid and CPR; porters are hired properly, with fair pay, proper gear and weighed loads, and everyone gets a daily health check on the mountain. About 5% of each trip funds schooling in a village near the mountain. When you climb with us, you climb with a crew that is looked after, and that is how you reach the summit with a clear conscience.
