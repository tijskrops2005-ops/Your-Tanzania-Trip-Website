Tanzania – a country of savannah where lions, zebras and giraffes roam, yet also one of long white beaches along the coast and on its islands. It is a developing nation, so not everything runs the way you may be used to at home. The facts and tips below should help you arrive well prepared for your trip!

Tanzania overview

Tanzania lies in East Africa, between the Indian Ocean and its neighbours Kenya, Mozambique, Congo, Malawi and Rwanda. Its 58 million inhabitants belong to more than 120 different tribes. The largest city is Dar es Salaam on the coast, home to 2.6 million people. The official capital, however, is the far smaller Dodoma in the centre of the country. Over 120 tribes live in Tanzania and together they speak more than 100 languages. The biggest groups are the Sukuma, Nyamwezi, Haya, Nyakyusa and Chaggastam, each numbering over a million members. No single tribe dominates, though, and that helps keep the political climate stable.

There are two official languages in Tanzania: English and Kiswahili. On top of that, many tribes speak their own mother tongue. Kiswahili is spoken not just in Tanzania but also (in part) in Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Mozambique and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The language has Bantu roots, with a number of English and Arabic words mixed in. Most Tanzanians speak Kiswahili above all, frequently their mother tongue too, and sometimes English.

Facts about Tanzania:

  • Capital: Dodoma
  • Currency: Tanzanian Shilling
  • Population: 58 million
  • Language: Kiswahili and English
  • Area: 886,000 km²
  • Water surface: 59,000 km²
  • Bordering countries: Kenya, Uganda, Congo, Rwanda, Burundi, Zambia, Malawi and Mozambique
  • Highest point: 5895 metres (and you can climb Kilimanjaro together with us!)
  • Time difference: during UK summer time 2 hrs ahead; during UK winter time 3 hrs ahead; 7-10 hrs ahead of the US
  • Best-known destinations: Serengeti National Park, Ngorongoro Crater, Kilimanjaro and Zanzibar

Safari in Tanzania

The word safari comes from Kiswahili and simply means travel. In the western world, though, an African safari is mostly linked to watching wild animals. The animals people want to see most are the Big Five: lions, elephants, buffaloes, leopards and rhinos. Yet Tanzania is home to countless other remarkable species as well as hundreds of beautiful, colourful birds. With Your Tanzania Trip you can see them all! Your private guide will gladly explain everything about the animals, birds and plants you come across on the way. For the safari itself you travel in our 4 × 4 Landcruiser Jeep with a fold-up roof. Because Your Tanzania Trip only runs private safaris, you don’t have to share the vehicle with anyone else. You choose when the safari begins and ends, and together with your guide you set the programme for the day. Should you suddenly fancy finishing the day by the pool, that’s perfectly fine. With a bit of luck you might even spot wild animals from there.

Visit local tribes

While on safari in Tanzania you can also pay a visit to a traditional tribe. The best-known tribe in the country is probably the Maasai, nomadic people who make their living from their cattle. They are recognised by their beautiful pearl jewellery, the long spears they carry and their red shukkas (blankets worn as clothing). Their semi-permanent huts – known as manyattas – are built from grass and dung. In their traditional dance, the aim is to jump as high as you can. The oldest tribe in Tanzania, the Hadzabe, lives around Lake Eyasi. Until recently they kept very much to themselves, but today they are willing to receive tourists. The Hadzabe still follow a traditional way of life and mainly live from hunting. Like the San and Xhosa further south, they speak a click language.

Worth knowing: animals are at their most active around sunrise and sunset. At those hours the temperatures in Tanzania are pleasant and the animals go out in search of food, so your chance of seeing them in action is greatest then. That said, there is still plenty of wildlife to be seen in the middle of the day.

What you need to know before a safari in Tanzania

Before you travel to Tanzania it helps to know what lies ahead. Here you will find the most useful travel information.

Visa for Tanzania

For entry into Tanzania you need a visa and a passport that remains valid for at least six months after your return. The visa can be arranged beforehand at the Tanzanian embassy in London/ Washington, where it costs 40 British pounds/ 50 US Dollar. Alternatively you can apply on arrival at the airport, in which case the fee is 50 US dollars. If you choose that route, keep a pen and $ 50 in cash to hand. Expect to wait about an hour. If you are entering Tanzania overland, it is wise to sort out the visa in advance so you avoid long queues at the border.

Do check with the embassy before you leave whether the rules above still apply!

Climate in Tanzania

Tanzania has a climate with two seasons, and the difference between them is small. Temperatures are pleasant year-round, although they vary with altitude. In the highlands it is generally cooler, with a marked gap between day and night temperatures, whereas the coast stays warm and humid at all times. Daytime temperatures can climb to 35°C /95°F.

There are two rainy seasons in Tanzania: the short rains from mid-October until the end of November, and the long rains from the end of March until the end of May. You can still travel to Tanzania during these months. Most roads remain passable, the parks are green and lush, and there are fewer visitors. Rain tends to fall in the afternoon, while the mornings are sunny. Heavy rain can occasionally make dirt roads in the parks impassable; when that happens we always look for another route!

Safety

Tanzania is a safe country for a holiday and has even been named the safest country in Africa! Politically it is stable. Even so, poverty remains relatively widespread, so it makes sense to observe a few rules. If you stick to the advice below, which really applies in any large European city too, you will be safe:

  • Never leave your passport, money or valuables lying around in your room
  • Store valuables in a safe or hand them in at reception, where they are kept in dedicated lockers
  • Never leave important items (money, your bag) unattended in the safari vehicle, not even briefly
  • Leave expensive jewellery at home. A (simple) necklace, watch or wedding ring is fine, but avoid anything flashy
  • Don’t walk the streets after dark
  • Keep an eye out for pickpockets during the day as well
  • If you need a taxi, only use registered taxi companies

Healthcare in Tanzania

Health care in Tanzania is of varying quality. Medical facilities in the big cities are fair to good, but waiting times are long, and prevention beats cure.

Vaccinations

It may be wise to get vaccinated before travelling to Tanzania. As we are not medically trained and guidelines can change over the year, we advise you to contact the health department well before departure for the latest information on which vaccinations may be required for Tanzania.

What about the yellow fever vaccine?

Details about the yellow fever vaccine are sometimes confusing. We would like to clear this up for you as far as entry into Tanzania is concerned.

Is a yellow fever vaccination needed if you fly directly from Europe or the United States to Tanzania?

The Tanzanian government only asks for proof of yellow fever vaccination on arrival when you travel from a country where yellow fever is a risk (see the list of high-risk countries here).

If, however, you transit through one of the high-risk countries for more than 12 hours, vaccination becomes compulsory. In any case we strongly advise you to consult your doctor, who can advise you further.

If your doctor advises against the vaccine for one of these reasons, you may still enter Tanzania from a high risk country with an official letter from the doctor. Present it to the immigration officer on arrival and the requirement is waived, so your trip continues as planned!

Under the World Health Organization’s classification, Tanzania counts as Yellow fever free.

Vaccination card and first aid kit

Make sure every vaccination is recorded in your vaccination card. If you don’t have one yet, your doctor can issue one. Don’t forget to pack it, as customs may ask to see it on arrival, and it also gives useful information should you need to see a doctor. We also advise bringing your own first aid kit to Tanzania, containing among other things plasters, (sterile) gauze, blister treatment, iodine and medicines for everyday complaints such as headaches and bowel problems. Sanitary towels and tampons are worth packing too, since they can be hard to find in some areas.

Dress code in Tanzania

Dress in Tanzania is casual. It is customary to cover your shoulders and to wear skirts and trousers that come below the knee. On Zanzibar most women also cover their hair. These customs are not applied very strictly, however, and the accommodation you stay in has no strict dress code, so shorts and skirts are fine there.

Because Tanzania lies south of the equator, the sun can be fierce. Too much of it may cause dehydration, nausea, dizziness or headaches, so wear a hat and dark sunglasses during the day. Sunscreen is essential as well.

Once the sun has set, long trousers (and perhaps long sleeves) are advisable because of the mosquitoes. Pack a jumper or jacket for early mornings and cooler evenings; the mountainous parts of Tanzania in particular get chilly. We also suggest comfortable (sports) shoes, plus swimwear for your stay on Zanzibar and for lodges with a swimming pool.

On safari, casual clothing is the norm, ideally cotton and in safari colours. We advise against dark or bright colours (black, blue, red) while on safari – they attract flies and insects. Shades that don’t stand out, such as khaki or olive green, work best.

Food

Meals and drinks in the lodges, camps and resorts of Tanzania are mainly European in style, with a choice of Tanzanian dishes alongside. In the morning there is a full English breakfast with eggs, bacon, tomatoes, cereal and delicious tropical fruit. Lunch is often a hot meal and dinner has three or more courses. Smaller lodges and camps generally serve a set menu, while the larger lodges tend to offer lunch and dinner as a buffet. The local beers (Kilimanjaro, Serengeti and Safari are the best known) are good, and there is usually a selection of wines. Tap water is not drinkable, which is why your room has a bottle or jug of purified drinking water; bottled water can also be bought at every lodge. Be sure to drink enough: in the Tanzanian heat you lose a lot of fluid without noticing. On safari days you get a packed lunch to eat at designated picnic spots along the route.

Money in Tanzania

Payments in Tanzania are made in the local currency, the Tanzanian Shilling. It comes in banknotes of 1000, 2000, 5000 and 10,000 Tsh and in coins of 50, 100, 200 and 500 Tsh.

Outside Tanzania the Tanzanian shilling cannot be bought or exchanged, so it’s best to bring cash (banknotes) in Euro or US dollars (banknotes from 2006). Some small change is handy too, in case you want to tip staff. The airport on arrival is the best place to exchange money.

ATMs are available at the international airports and in the bigger villages and the city. They don’t always work, unfortunately, so plan ahead.

Credit cards are accepted too. Mastercard works everywhere, whereas Visa and American Express are not taken in all places. Lodges accept Euros, US-Dollars or credit card, and will also exchange US-Dollar or Euro for Tanzanian shilling at a (generally) average rate. In the camps, paying by credit card can occasionally be a problem because of the remote location and poor connections.

Tips

Tips are not compulsory in Tanzania. Wages are not high, however, and staff are grateful for them. Our advice is to tip only when you are happy with the service. Usually these are small amounts, for instance $ 1 per person for whoever carries your suitcase to your hotel room. On check-out you can often leave a general tip for all staff as well; reception normally has a box set aside for this.

At the end of the safari you can tip your private guide. Naturally a tip should come from the heart and is never an obligation. As a rule of thumb we suggest 10 USD per day per person in your group for the length of the safari, since the guide is at your disposal the whole time. Our guides are paid decent salaries, but tips are often put aside as savings for the extended family (helping a cousin complete school, for example).

Photography and video

Many travellers hope to capture beautiful nature shots during a game drive. If that includes you, bear the following in mind:

  • Pack plenty of memory cards and spare batteries, as these are scarce or unavailable in Tanzania
  • Take lens caps, cleaning cloths and a close-fitting camera bag to keep dust off your camera
  • A UV or light filter is useful in the highlands and protects the lens
  • For photographing wildlife, a zoom lens (300 mm, for example) is advisable
  • Bear in mind when charging batteries that some tented camps only run a generator for a few hours a day, and the voltage is not stable
  • The use of drones is not permitted
  • Always ask before photographing local people. Cameras are more familiar these days, yet not everyone appreciates them. Asking first is a sign of respect!

Phone calls from Tanzania

Mobile phone coverage is available in many places, though there are spots where there isn’t any. Lodges and camps inside the national parks nearly always have a hand radio, so they can be reached in an emergency, but that link is not meant for long calls home. Tanzania’s country code is +255.

Electricity

Voltage in Tanzania differs from Europe. Hotels run on 220-240 volts, though this can fluctuate. Sockets are of the three-pin (English) type, so bringing an adapter is advisable. Note that in many lodges and camps electricity is not available around the clock.

Insurance

Travel insurance

We advise all our customers to take out good travel insurance. Besides medical costs, travel insurance usually also covers damaged or lost luggage.

Travel cancellation insurance

Cancellation insurance is something we recommend as well. Some policies only pay out up to a certain sum if you cancel – do check this carefully!

Flying Doctors Insurance

Since the 1950s the AMREF Flying Doctors have flown medical evacuations in Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda. This Flying Doctor Service is the only flying ambulance with its own doctors and pilots on standby 24 hours a day. Flying Doctors Insurance is an add-on to your normal travel insurance and is not compulsory, but in an emergency they organise immediate evacuation from remote areas to the hospital in Nairobi. The cover costs 25 € per person for a safari of at most fourteen days in Kenya and Tanzania.

Public holidays in Tanzania

Besides the familiar Christian holidays, Tanzania observes these national holidays:

  • 12 January: Revolution Day on Zanzibar
  • April 26th: Union Day
  • May 1st: Labor Day
  • 7 July: Saba Saba (farmers’ day)
  • October 14th: Nyerere Day
  • December 9th: Independence Day
