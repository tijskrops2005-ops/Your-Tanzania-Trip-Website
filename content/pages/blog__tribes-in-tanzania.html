How Many Tribes in Tanzania? An Insight into Tanzania's Rich Tribal Heritage

30 November 2023

With over 100 separate tribes and ethnic groups, Tanzania is famous for its rich cultural variety. Most of Tanzania’s tribes are of Bantu origin, accounting for roughly 95% of the country’s population.

The remaining tribes are made up of Nilotic speakers and the descendants of indigenous hunter-gatherers. A small share of Tanzanians have Arabic or Indian roots, living mainly in the coastal regions, Dar es Salaam and Zanzibar.

A short historical overview

Tanzania’s tribal history goes back to the Khoisan-speaking hunter-gatherers, who are thought to be the country’s first inhabitants. Over the centuries a succession of different tribes moved into the area, most of them Bantu-speaking peoples from West and Central Africa. They brought ironworking and new farming skills with them, shaping the ethnic make-up of Tanzanian society to a large degree.

Major Tribes in Tanzania

Some tribes in Tanzania still follow a traditional way of life, mainly in the villages. In the towns and cities (and in certain other places too), many tribes have mixed and people lead more modern lives. They still see themselves as members of their tribe, but they live in ordinary houses in neighbourhoods shared with other tribes and religions.

The major tribes in Tanzania include:

The Sukuma

Tanzania’s largest ethnic group is the Sukuma, who live in the north-western regions of the country, mainly in the administrative districts of Mwanza and Shinyanga. There are also Sukuma communities in the provinces of Tabora, Dodoma and Singida.

Alongside the Chagga, as well as the Indian and Arab minorities, they are the most powerful and influential tribes in politics and business.

Little has been written down about Sukuma history, but their ancestors are thought to have come from Bantu-speaking peoples in West Africa. They moved to the part of Tanzania where they live now over the course of several centuries.

Traditionally farmers, the Sukuma mostly grow crops and work on a small scale. They are famous for the snake dance, the ‘Bugobogobo’, which forms a key part of many of their healing and spiritual rituals.

The Nyamwezi

In western Tanzania, the Nyamwezi are the second-largest ethnic group, after the Sukuma. The name Nyamwezi means “people of the moon,” a reference to their age-old tradition of worshipping the moon.

The Nyamwezi are thought to have settled in west-central Tanzania during the 17th century. By the early 19th century the tribe was made up of several kingdoms, such as Unyanyembe, Ulyankhulu and Urambo.

Unyanyembe carried particular weight because it ruled Tabora, an important trading city, and maintained close ties with the Arabs of Zanzibar. All through their history, the Nyamwezi took part in exploration and long-distance trade.

Ancestral spirits were central to daily life in traditional Nyamwezi society. People believed their ancestors could influence the living for better or worse, and a range of rituals and forms of worship were meant to keep these spirits content.

The Chagga

The Chagga, or Wachaga in Swahili, are a Bantu ethnic group whose home is the Kilimanjaro Region of Tanzania.

They make up the country’s third-largest ethnic group. Before colonisation, the Chagga were traditionally organised into independent states on the slopes of Mount Kilimanjaro.

Known historically as Chaggaland, or Uchaggani in Swahili, this area was made up of a group of Bantu kingdoms that predated colonial rule.

The cultural history of the Chagga is rich and includes local traditional rulers called ‘Mangi.’ Their homesteads, known as Kihamba, are family plots of land handed down from one generation to the next.

The Chagga are an influential tribe, and Arusha / Moshi an influential region. A number of people still follow a traditional way of life, yet many have also built lovely, modern homes.

The Maasai

The Maasai, on the other hand (along with a few other tribes such as the hadzabe), mostly still live in the traditional way. Even in the city they usually wear their traditional clothing, keep together and speak their own language.

The Maasai are thought to originate from the Nile Valley in North Africa. From around the 15th century they began migrating south, finally arriving in what is now Kenya and Tanzania. The Maasai were at the height of their power in the late 17th and 18th centuries, when they controlled large parts of East Africa’s landscapes.

Known for their distinctive dress and customs, the tribe keeps to a semi-nomadic way of life that depends largely on herding livestock.

You can easily recognise the Maasai by their clothing (Shuka), their beaded jewellery and their striking tradition of body modification, such as piercing and stretching the ears.

One key cultural event is the warrior initiation ceremony called ‘Eunoto,’ in which young Maasai men move into elderhood and take on new responsibilities in the tribe.

The Hehe

Famous for their warrior traditions and their resilience, the Hehe tribe are the dominant group in the Iringa Region of south-central Tanzania.

The Hehe came into being in the 19th century, when several older communities joined together under their well-known leader, Chief Mkwawa. They united mainly to fight off outside threats, above all slave traders and European colonists.

This part of their cultural heritage stands out and is still remembered today.

In social and economic terms, the Hehe mostly work in farming and keeping animals.

The Gogo

The Gogo tribe is a Central Bantu ethnic group living in the Dodoma Region of central Tanzania. They belong to the wider Bantu peoples who spread across Africa roughly 2,000 to 3,000 years ago.

The Gogo are every bit as varied and lively as their history. They are famous for their traditional music, played on local instruments such as the Zeze (with two strings), many kinds of drums and the distinctive Ndono, a one-string instrument made from a calabash.

The Haya

The Haya live in the Kagera region close to the shores of Lake Victoria and have a rich cultural history.

Called Wahaya in Swahili, they are thought to descend from a group of iron-working farmers who spread out into various parts of Africa. This is fascinating, as these Iron Age people shaped a future that led to the Haya people of today.

According to experts, they started making steel, or hard metal, as long as 2000 years ago. In other words, these early people had worked out very clever methods of combining metals to produce it before many others in the world.

The Haya are known for their distinctive buildings, including big roundhouses of mud and thatch known as the mushonge.

 

Other Tribes you may have heard of

The Zaramo

Known for its strongly matrilineal society, the Zaramo tribe lives mainly in the coastal region of Tanzania, with Dar es Salaam, Tanzania’s largest city, at its centre. The Zaramo follow a mix of traditional religions and Islam, which has been widespread in the region since the 18th century.

The Zaramo are farmers and fishermen who grow staple crops such as cassava, beans, rice and corn. Apart from farming, the tribe is skilled in arts and crafts.

Their art includes wood carving and pottery. They also perform a dance called Mdundiko.

The Makonde

The Makonde tribe came from Mozambique and settled in southern Tanzania, especially in the Mtwara region.

The Makonde are famous worldwide for their woodcarving skills and detailed work in ebony, showing human and animal figures as well as abstract designs.

Lineage in the tribe is matrilineal, and the woman’s side of the family carries special importance. Each year the Makonde also hold an initiation ceremony called Nguvumali, in which young boys and girls become adults through traditional teachings and symbolic rituals.

The Pare

The Pare tribe lives in north-east Tanzania and is split into two subgroups, the Asu and the Chasu. The Pare mountains suit their farming way of life perfectly, and they are known for growing coffee, maize, beans and bananas.

The Pare have a distinctive social structure made up of small independent polities, each led by a hereditary ruler, which shows how complex their political organisation’s set-up is.

One important cultural highlight is the Ijanja dance, a traditional performance combining rhythmic movement and sound into a captivating show.

The Makua

The Makua tribe lives mainly in Mozambique, but it also has a sizeable presence in Tanzania, especially in the Mtwara region.

Traditionally, their society is patriarchal and places strong emphasis on the male line. In cultural terms, the Makua are known for weaving: the men make mats and the women make baskets.

Their music and dance are also well known, with intricate rhythms and melodies woven into their performances.

The Zigua

The Zigua live in Tanzania’s Tanga region and are an ethnic group with strong farming traditions, mostly growing rice, millet and cassava, and fishing regularly along the coast.

In the past, the Zigua took an important part in long-distance trade on the caravan routes linking the East African coast with Lake Tanganyika.

Music and dance are prominent in the traditional rituals and ceremonies of Zigua culture. One example is the “Ukala” dance, a dance about hunting. Using instruments such as rattles and drums, the dancers captivate their audience with a mix of rhythmic sounds and chanting.

Lesser-Known Tribes

Hadza and Sandawe

Regarded as indigenous tribes of Tanzania, the Hadza and Sandawe still live as hunter-gatherers. They are known for their distinctive ‘clicking’ languages, which share linguistic traits with the Khoisan languages of the definitive San people of Southern Africa.

Iraqw

Living apart in the cool highlands of north-central Tanzania, the Iraqw tribe has kept its own Cushitic language, which differs from the Bantu, Nilotic and Khoisan languages that dominate in Tanzania. The Iraqw are mainly farmers, using their knowledge of the region’s fertile volcanic soil to grow a wide range of crops.

Wrapping Up

Every tribe in Tanzania brings its own cultural, historical and social character to the country. Together they show the rich diversity Tanzania has to offer, reflecting a truly African tapestry of tribal cultures that is not just surviving but flourishing within the borders of this East African nation.
