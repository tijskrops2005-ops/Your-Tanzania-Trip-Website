The Ndutu Region belongs to the Ngorongoro Conservation Area and runs on into the unfenced southern section of the Serengeti National Park. Resident wildlife is abundant throughout the year, with elephants, hyenas, giraffes and big cats all living here.

Great Migration in the region

Around the end of December the Great Migration normally reaches Ndutu, bringing roughly 2 million animals with it. Wildebeest, zebras and gazelles assemble here to calve and stay in the area until April. Between December and March, thousands of calves arrive every single day. The grass is packed with nutrients for the young, and because it is short, the adults can see predators coming from far away. That is why Ndutu is a safe place for young zebras and wildebeest to grow up. It is a beautiful region with a very high density of wildlife. The calving season draws plenty of predators onto the open plains: lions, cheetahs, leopards, hyenas and large packs of wild dogs!

Lake Ndutu

The finest game viewing is found around Lake Ndutu, where the huge herds gather. This shallow alkaline lake lies on the eastern edge of the Serengeti National Park. In 1973, an archaeological dig of roughly 140 square metres on the lakeshore uncovered the Ndutu cranium together with a number of tool remains dating back more than 500.000 years.
These days the lake provides water for the animals and sits in the same basin as Lake Masek, a short way to the east, though that lake lies within the Ngorongoro Conservation Area.
The woodland and granite kopjes surrounding Lake Ndutu hold plenty of game outside the migration months as well.

Excursions around Ndutu

Olduvai Gorge (the Maasai spelling is Oldupai, meaning “the place of the wild sisal”) ranks among the most important paleoanthropological sites on earth. This steep-sided ravine in the Great Rift Valley is around 48 km long. It lies 25 km from Lake Ndutu and Lake Masek and cuts through Pleistocene lake-bed sediments to a depth of as much as 90 m. The earliest evidence of our human ancestors was unearthed here, and those finds have been invaluable for understanding early human evolution. The hundreds of fossils (bones) found at the site are millions of years old.

There is also a small museum on site where you can learn more about the remarkable excavations and finds made here.

Best time to visit

The best time to visit the Ndutu Region runs from late December, when the Great Migration herds begin to arrive from the north, until April, when the millions of wildebeest, zebra and gazelle set off northwards once more. During the first months of the year, peaking in February and March, the wildebeest give birth on the grassy plains.
